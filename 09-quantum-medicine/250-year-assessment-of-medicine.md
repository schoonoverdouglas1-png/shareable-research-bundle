# AN ASSESSMENT OF MEDICINE OVER THE LAST 250 YEARS

*Prepared for the QUANTUM MEDICINE manuscript — a chapter/appendix of theory.
This is manuscript material. It is not medical advice to any person.*

---

## THE METHOD: A ONE-TIME ASSUMPTION

This assessment begins with a single deliberate act, taken once and then set aside:
assume that ALL of medicine could be wrong. Not as a conclusion — as a tool. The way
a diagnostician must sometimes wipe the slate clean to see the patient clearly, this
assessment wipes the slate to see two and a half centuries of medicine clearly.

Methodic doubt is not contrarianism. The contrarian decides the answer before the
inquiry. Methodic doubt decides nothing. It asks, era by era: what survived because
it works? What collapsed when tested? What persists not by evidence but by inertia,
incentive, or fear? Credit is given where the record earns it. Nothing is spared
where the record condemns it.

The window is 1776 to 2026. Two hundred and fifty years.

---

## ERA I: 1776–1850 — HEROIC MEDICINE

The medicine of this era was confident, violent, and mostly wrong about treatment —
while quietly building the observational foundation everything later would stand on.

**Load-bearing (survived because it works):**
- *Vaccination.* Edward Jenner's 1796 smallpox work — cowpox inoculation preventing
  smallpox — is the single most load-bearing event of the era. It worked before
  anyone knew why, and it has never stopped working. Every vaccine since descends
  from it.
- *Clinical observation and autopsy.* The Paris Clinical School's method — correlate
  the living patient's signs with the dead patient's anatomy — became the permanent
  grammar of diagnosis. Observation outlived every theory of the era.
- *The numerical method.* Pierre Charles Alexandre Louis published his statistical
  attack on bloodletting in 1835 (English translation 1836). Studying 77 pneumonia
  cases, he found 44 percent of patients bled early in illness died, versus 25
  percent of those bled later. He had invented clinical epidemiology: count the
  outcomes, trust the count over the doctrine. The method survived. Most of what it
  has killed since deserved killing.

**Collapsed under testing:**
- *Heroic depletion therapy.* Bleeding, purging, blistering, and mercury-laden
  calomel — championed by men like Benjamin Rush — were the standard of care.
  George Washington died in December 1799 after his physicians drew roughly 24
  ounces of his blood along with blisterings and emetics for a throat infection.
  Louis's numbers began the end of it, but note the lag honestly: bloodletting
  persisted in common practice for decades after the evidence turned. Patients in
  the 1870s still had to be talked OUT of being bled. Doctrine outlives data by a
  generation. Remember that pattern; it repeats.
- *Humoral theory as therapy.* The idea that disease was imbalance of humors, to be
  corrected by violent evacuation, produced a century of confident harm.

**Persists by inertia:**
- The authority structure: the physician as unquestioned figure, the patient as
  passive recipient. The treatments died. The hierarchy survived into the next
  century.

## ERA II: 1850–1900 — GERMS, FILTH, AND THE SCALPEL

This is the era where medicine stopped being mostly wrong about *causes* and started
being right — while remaining dangerously wrong about *itself*.

**Load-bearing:**
- *Germ theory.* Pasteur in the 1860s, Koch's postulates (1876–1884): specific
  microbes cause specific diseases. This is the load-bearing wall of all modern
  medicine. Nothing since has knocked it down.
- *Sanitation and epidemiology.* John Snow's 1854 Broad Street pump investigation
  proved cholera traveled in water, not miasma — by mapping deaths house by house,
  the first great act of shoe-leather epidemiology. Florence Nightingale's Crimean
  sanitation work (1854–56) cut hospital death rates by cleanliness and statistics;
  she was arguably the first to weaponize data visualization for medicine.
- *Antisepsis.* Joseph Lister's 1867 carbolic-acid surgical antisepsis, built on
  Pasteur, made surgery survivable. Before it, a surgeon's ward was a charnel house.
- *Anesthesia.* Ether anesthesia (Morton, 1846, just over the era's border) made
  modern surgery possible at all.

**Collapsed under testing:**
- *Miasma theory.* Disease as bad air. Snow killed it with a map. It took decades
  to die fully — another instance of the lag.
- *The Semmelweis delay — filed here as evidence, not just history.* In May 1847,
  Ignaz Semmelweis ordered chlorine handwashing in Vienna's First Obstetric Clinic
  and drove maternal mortality from roughly 10 percent of births to roughly 1
  percent. The medical establishment rejected him — partly because germ theory did
  not yet exist to explain him, partly because his finding indicted doctors
  themselves as the carriers. He died in an asylum in 1865. Lister vindicated the
  principle in 1867. The United States did not issue national hand-hygiene
  guidelines until the 1980s — more than a century later. This is the single
  clearest proof in the record that medicine's authority structure can suppress a
  true, life-saving finding for generations. The author's thesis about institutional
  resistance is not paranoia here. It is documented.

**Persists by inertia:**
- The guild reflex: when evidence threatens the profession's self-image, the
  profession attacks the evidence first. Semmelweis is the monument. The reflex did
  not die with him.

## ERA III: 1900–1950 — THE INDUSTRIALIZATION OF CURE

Medicine became an industry in this era: standardized, regulated, and capable of
both miracles and mass-produced error.

**Load-bearing:**
- *The Flexner reform of education.* Abraham Flexner's 1910 report for the Carnegie
  Foundation surveyed 155 North American medical schools and demanded laboratory
  science, university standards, and clinical training. Roughly half the schools
  merged or closed. American medicine became scientifically literate. That part
  worked — and the honest assessment credits it fully.
- *Chemotherapy's dawn.* Ehrlich's Salvarsan (1909–10) for syphilis — the "magic
  bullet" idea. Domagk's Prontosil (1935), the first sulfonamide; his own young
  daughter was among its first cures. Then Fleming's 1928 penicillin observation,
  industrialized by Florey, Chain, and Heatley in 1940–41, Nobel Prize 1945.
  Antibiotics are the second load-bearing wall after germ theory.
- *Regulation born of catastrophe.* The 1937 Elixir Sulfanilamide disaster — over
  100 dead, many children, from an untested toxic solvent — forced the U.S. Food,
  Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938: proof of safety before sale. Regulation, at its
  origin, was a response to corpses, not a bureaucratic whim.
- *Insulin (1921).* Diabetes went from a death sentence to a managed condition.
- *The sanatorium method.* Before antibiotics, tuberculosis was fought with rest,
  air, nutrition, and relentless sputum monitoring — smear, culture, watch. Crude,
  but it was systematic observation, and it held the line until streptomycin
  (Waksman, 1943) arrived.

**Collapsed under testing:**
- *Unregulated proprietary medicine.* The patent-medicine era died by law and by
  laboratory, and good riddance.
- *Therapeutic nihilism's opposite — therapeutic credulity.* The early antibiotic
  era bred the assumption that every infection had a chemical answer, an assumption
  the next era would pay for in resistance.

**Persists by inertia — and the Flexner shadow:**
- Here the assessment must be two-handed, because the author's framework demands
  honesty about institutions. Flexner's reform worked scientifically AND did
  documented damage: five of the seven Black medical schools in the United States
  were closed in its wake, with consequences for physician diversity and access
  that persist today. Homeopathy, eclectic medicine, and other heterodox schools
  were shuttered or absorbed — some deserved it on the evidence, some were closed
  by standard rather than by trial. The reform centralized authority in a way that
  made the next century's dogma possible. Both things are true: the standard rose,
  and the gate narrowed. A serious assessment records both.

## ERA IV: 1950–2000 — THE PHARMA CENTURY AND THE REVERSAL LITERATURE

The era of maximum confidence — and the era when medicine began documenting its own
errors systematically.

**Load-bearing:**
- *Vaccines at scale.* Salk (1955) and Sabin (1961) polio vaccines; the
  near-eradication of a crippling disease within a generation. The load-bearing
  tradition of Jenner, continued.
- *Proof-of-efficacy regulation.* The thalidomide catastrophe — thousands of
  birth defects worldwide — was narrowly kept out of the United States by FDA
  reviewer Frances Kelsey, who refused approval for lack of safety data. Congress
  responded with the Kefauver-Harris Amendments, signed October 10, 1962: drugs
  must prove safety AND efficacy before marketing. This is regulation as
  life-saving infrastructure, and the author's pro-regulation instincts have a
  genuine monument here. Kelsey received the President's Distinguished Federal
  Civilian Service Award from Kennedy himself.
- *Imaging.* CT (1970s) and MRI (1980s) let physicians see inside the living body
  without opening it. Diagnostics were transformed permanently.
- *Intensive care, transplantation, statins.* The ICU, organ transplantation, and
  lipid-lowering therapy each moved mortality curves that had not moved in
  millennia.

**Collapsed under testing — the reversal record:**
- This era produced the evidence base for medicine's capacity to be wrong at
  scale. Physicians Prasad and Cifu documented 146 established practices reversed
  in a single decade's literature. The roster: hormone replacement therapy for
  prevention (the Women's Health Initiative, 2002 — worse than nothing for
  cardiovascular outcomes), coronary stents for stable angina (no better than
  medical management), Vioxx (withdrawn 2004, cardiovascular deaths), vertebroplasty
  for compression fractures, routine pulmonary-artery catheters in shock,
  arthroscopic knee surgery for degenerative meniscal tears. Each was adopted on
  mechanism and eminence. Each fell to randomized trials. The pattern is not
  anecdotal; it is the literature.
- *Lobotomy.* Tens of thousands performed mid-century, a Nobel Prize awarded for
  it (Moniz, 1949), then abandoned as barbarism within a generation. The
  profession's highest honor, attached to a practice it now disowns. File that.

**Persists by inertia:**
- *Antibiotic overuse and resistance.* The miracle of Era III, squandered by
  volume — prescribed for viral illness, fed to livestock — breeding the
  resistance crisis. The drug works. The stewardship failed.
- *Fee-for-service overutilization.* When revenue follows procedures, procedures
  multiply. Incentive, not evidence, drives a measurable share of care.
- *Defensive medicine.* Here the assessment engages the author's thesis directly,
  with the numbers on the table. Defensive practice is real: in one Cleveland
  Clinic study, 28 percent of hospital orders were rated at least partially
  defensive; 96 percent of surveyed orthopedic surgeons admitted defensive
  practice. But the cost estimates must be stated honestly: the most defensible
  accounting (Mello and colleagues, Health Affairs) puts total malpractice-system
  costs, including defensive medicine, at about $55.6 billion per year — roughly
  2.4 percent of U.S. health spending. Other estimates run higher ($46–210
  billion), but the rigorous ones cluster low, and tort reform's demonstrated
  savings are modest. The author's instinct — that lawsuit fear distorts medicine —
  is confirmed by the evidence. The author's stronger claim — that ending lawsuits
  would automatically transform quality and cost — is NOT confirmed by the
  evidence. Defensive medicine is a real tax on the system, not the system's
  master key. Both statements belong in the book.

## ERA V: 2000–2026 — THE GENOME, THE IMMUNE SYSTEM, AND THE MACHINE

**Load-bearing:**
- *The Human Genome Project.* Declared complete April 14, 2003 — 92 percent of
  the genome, finished gapless in 2022. Three billion letters, read. The map on
  which the next century of medicine will be drawn.
- *Immunotherapy.* Allison and Honjo's checkpoint-blockade work (CTLA-4, PD-1) —
  Nobel 2018 — turned the immune system itself into a cancer therapy. Ipilimumab
  (2011), pembrolizumab and nivolumab (2014): metastatic melanoma went from death
  sentence to durable remission for a real fraction of patients. An entirely new
  principle of therapy, established in the author's lifetime.
- *mRNA vaccines.* The COVID-19 vaccines (2020) proved a platform: sequence the
  pathogen, synthesize the message, immunize at pandemic speed.

**Collapsed or cracking:**
- *The replication crisis.* Ioannidis's 2005 analysis — "Why Most Published
  Research Findings Are False" — demonstrated mathematically what the reversal
  literature showed empirically: small studies, flexible methods, financial
  interests, and publication bias mean most claimed findings are more likely
  false than true. The doctrine's own printing press was indicted by one of its
  own. This belongs in any honest assessment and directly supports the author's
  demand for a filter.
- *AI in medicine — promise, concentrated narrowly.* By late 2025 the FDA had
  authorized over 1,350 AI-enabled devices — but 77 percent were in radiology,
  and the overwhelming majority cleared through the 510(k) "substantially
  equivalent" pathway, with only a small fraction undergoing prospective clinical
  testing. Approval is not adoption, and authorization is not proof of benefit.
  The machine reads images well. Whether it heals is still an open trial.

**Persists by inertia:**
- Publication bias, eminence-based practice, and the reimbursement structures of
  Era IV — carried forward on momentum.

## THE DOCTRINE AS THEOLOGY: TESTING THE AUTHOR'S CHARGE

The author directs that post-1900 medicine's written doctrine be examined as
theology-like — dogma transmitted by teaching — and tested against seven causes
of error: wrongful teachings, bad thinking, incorrect knowledge, lack of
knowledge, lack of inspiration, war, and espionage. This section takes that
charge seriously and tests each cause against the record. The forced conclusion —
"increasingly wrong" — is not adopted. It is tested.

**1. Wrongful teachings.** Documented. The Flexner closures are the prime exhibit:
standards rose, and five of seven Black medical schools were destroyed with
lasting consequences. Semmelweis was destroyed by teaching, not by evidence.
Verdict: CONFIRMED as a real mechanism, with real victims.

**2. Bad thinking.** Documented. Eminence-based medicine — "the professor said
so" — installed stents, hormones, and vertebroplasties without randomized
evidence. The reversal literature (146 documented reversals) is the autopsy
report. Verdict: CONFIRMED.

**3. Incorrect knowledge.** Documented wherever reversals occurred. But note the
direction honestly: incorrect knowledge was REPLACED by correct knowledge through
the trial mechanism. The system that produced the error also produced the
correction. Verdict: CONFIRMED as episodic, not as the system's character.

**4. Lack of knowledge.** Documented — and this is where the author's demand for
humility is strongest. Bloodletting persisted half a century past Louis.
Handwashing waited a century past Semmelweis for national guidelines. The lag
between evidence and practice is measured in decades, repeatedly. Verdict:
CONFIRMED, and it is the most expensive of the seven.

**5. Lack of inspiration.** Harder to test, but the record offers a proxy: the
therapies that transformed mortality — sanitation, vaccines, antibiotics,
anesthesia — came disproportionately from outsiders, accidents, and peripheral
observers (a country doctor with cowpox, a moldy petri dish, a pump handle map).
The center rarely innovates; it certifies. Verdict: PLAUSIBLE, supported by the
pattern of outsider breakthroughs.

**6 & 7. War and espionage — the spy game against ourselves.** Here the record
is damning, specific, and declassified:

- *Unit 731.* Japan's biological-warfare program murdered approximately 12,000
  people in direct experimentation. In 1947, the United States — by MacArthur's
  authority — granted Shirō Ishii and his staff full immunity from war-crimes
  prosecution in exchange for the program's data. A joint
  State-War-Navy assessment stated the data's value to national security "far
  outweighed" the value of prosecution. Ishii later lectured at Camp Detrick.
  He died a free man. The data went into the American bioweapons program.
- *Operation Paperclip.* Roughly 1,600 German scientists were brought to the
  United States, including men from medicine and biological/chemical weapons
  work — among them Kurt Blome, Walter Schreiber, Hubertus Strughold, and Erich
  Traub. Paperwork was altered to conceal Nazi affiliations.
- *Fort Detrick.* The U.S. ran an offensive biological-weapons program from
  1943 until President Nixon unilaterally ended it on November 25, 1969 — at
  which point it was spending $300 million a year. The arsenal was destroyed
  through 1972–73.
- *MKUltra.* From 1953 to 1973, the CIA dosed unwitting Americans and others
  with LSD, mescaline, and worse in pursuit of mind control. Frank Olson died
  in 1953 after being dosed without his knowledge. Director Helms ordered the
  files destroyed in 1973; the Church Committee (1975) reconstructed enough to
  force President Ford's 1976 executive order banning non-consensual human
  experimentation by intelligence agencies.
- *Tuskegee.* From 1932 to 1972, the U.S. Public Health Service studied
  untreated syphilis in Black men in Alabama — withholding penicillin after it
  became the standard cure.

The author's phrase — that we "played a spy game against ourselves" — has
genuine anchors in this record. Intelligence and war incentives did distort,
bury, and weaponize medical knowledge. Verdict: CONFIRMED as real, documented,
and shameful.

**But the forced conclusion fails its own test.** "Increasingly wrong" cannot
survive the mortality record. Over these 250 years, human life expectancy
roughly doubled; infectious disease went from the leading killer of humankind
to a managed risk in much of the world; smallpox — the greatest killer in
human history — was eradicated entirely, by the vaccine tradition this
assessment traces from Jenner. A system that was "increasingly wrong" does not
produce those curves. The honest verdict on the seven causes: each is real,
each did damage, and none describes the whole. Medicine got enormously more
RIGHT across 250 years — while remaining, at every step, capable of confident,
institutional, well-funded error. That is the true shape of the record, and the
book should carry it exactly that way. The two-pass filter the author designed
is the right instrument for exactly this record: it would have caught the
bloodletting, caught the stents, caught the hormones — and it would have kept
the vaccines, the sanitation, and the antibiotics. A filter that keeps Jenner
and discards Rush is a filter calibrated correctly.

## FINAL RECKONING

**SURVIVED — because it works, tested and retested:**
Vaccination (1796 → present). Germ theory. Sanitation and epidemiology.
Antisepsis. Anesthesia. Antibiotics. Proof-of-efficacy regulation (1938, 1962).
The randomized trial. The numerical method — count the outcomes. Imaging.
Immunotherapy. The Latin of the prescription pad, still written every day.

**FELL — adopted in confidence, abandoned in evidence:**
Heroic bleeding and purging. Miasma. Unregulated patent medicine. Lobotomy.
Hormone replacement for prevention. Stents for stable angina. Vioxx.
Vertebroplasty. Routine pulmonary-artery catheters. The doctrine that eminence
equals evidence.

**PERSISTS BY INERTIA, INCENTIVE, OR FEAR:**
Antibiotic overuse breeding resistance. Fee-for-service overutilization.
Defensive medicine — real, measurable, and smaller than its legend. The
decades-long lag between evidence and practice. Publication bias. The guild
reflex that punished Semmelweis and has never fully retired.

---

## CLOSING VERDICT

Two hundred and fifty years of medicine, weighed with the slate wiped clean:

Medicine was wrong about treatment for most of the first century, right about
causes by the second, miraculous and industrial by the third, and is now
powerful enough that its errors are studied the way its cures once were. The
arc is not "increasingly wrong." The arc is: increasingly powerful, decreasingly
excused. Power without humility is what produced the bloodletting, the
reversals, the buried data, and the immunity deals. The filter the author
proposes — science first, Hippocrates second, publish what survives — is aimed
at exactly the right target: not medicine's knowledge, which is the most
successful body of practical knowledge humans have ever assembled, but
medicine's *certainty*, which has always been the dangerous part.

Do the right thing, and the lawsuits cease because there is nothing left to
sue over. That is the author's theme. This assessment finds it pointed in the
right direction — with one correction the book should carry honestly: lawsuits
are one distortion among several, and ending them would improve medicine at the
margin, not remake it by itself. The full cure the author seeks requires the
whole filter, not one clause of it.
